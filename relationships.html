Understand common patterns

Relationship guides

A guide can offer language and options. It cannot tell you whether to stay, leave, forgive, trust or contact someone.

Attachment protest

When a connection feels threatened, someone may try urgently to restore contact, attention or reassurance: repeated messages, checking for clues, indirect tests, angry words or withdrawal.

What may be underneath?

Sometimes fear of rejection, uncertainty, loneliness or an unmet need for reassurance. The same behaviour can have other causes, including actual unreliability or poor communication. Do not infer a person's attachment pattern from one episode.

What can I try?
  • Separate the observable fact from the story you fear may be true.
  • Name the need: reassurance, clarity, time together or a mutually agreed reply expectation.
  • Make one direct, respectful request rather than testing, threatening or repeatedly contacting.
  • Respect the other person's stated boundaries. A request is not a right to constant access.

Anxious attachment patterns

Some people become especially alert to signs of rejection or distance and may seek frequent reassurance. Attachment is dimensional and may vary across relationships and situations, not a permanent identity.

What may help?

Build more than one source of support; notice triggers without shaming yourself; practise clear requests; keep routines and interests outside the relationship; consider individual counselling if fear or checking disrupts daily life.

Distance and emotional shutdown

Some people cope with relationship stress by reducing emotional contact, going quiet or needing space. Taking space can be healthy; disappearing without explanation or using silence to punish can harm trust.

What may help?

Say what is happening, ask for a pause if needed, and agree a realistic time to return. Do not force a conversation when someone is overwhelmed.

The pursue–withdraw loop

One person pushes for closeness or resolution while the other withdraws. Each response can intensify the other. This is a dyadic interaction pattern, not a personality diagnosis.

How to interrupt it safely

Agree on a pause with a clear return point. Each person takes responsibility for their own behaviour. Return to one issue, describe its impact, listen and agree one small action. Use this only where both people can participate freely.

Conflict habits and repair

Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling are educational labels for destructive conflict behaviours, not a diagnostic test or a way to score a partner.

Try a repair conversation

Choose a calm time. Describe one observable event, say how it affected you, name what matters and make a specific request. Listen back to what you heard. Own your part without accepting blame for threats or abuse.

Boundaries, consent and digital trust

Healthy boundaries cover time, privacy, friendships, location sharing, social media, sex, money and personal space. Consent can be withdrawn. Jealousy does not create a right to monitor or control another person.

Questions to discuss

What communication feels reasonable? What stays private? What do we each agree about devices, location sharing and social media? Can either person say no without punishment? Agreements should be mutual and freely chosen.

Breakups and missing someone

Separation can activate searching, protest, grief and urges to restore contact. These feelings do not mean you should resume a relationship or that the other person must respond.

What may help?

Respect explicit no-contact boundaries. Reduce repeated checking if it keeps reopening distress. Lean on trusted people, keep basic routines, write an unsent note, and seek individual support if everyday functioning or safety is affected.

Family pressure and difficult relationships

Family roles, culture, finances and expectations can make boundaries harder. You can care about family while still having limits around privacy, insults, pressure, money and personal decisions.

Try one clear boundary

State the limit, what you will do if it is crossed, and keep the consequence within your control. If setting a boundary could trigger retaliation, prioritise private support first.

What respectful connection can look like

Respect, consent, honesty, room for friendships and interests, privacy, shared decisions and the ability to disagree without fear. No relationship is conflict-free; repair and accountability matter.

When to seek support

Consider individual counselling when a pattern feels stuck or distress is affecting daily life. Couples counselling is not the default route when fear, coercion or violence is present.

Research report · appendix of 40 concepts

Forty relationship concepts, explained without labels.

This reference follows the 40-term appendix in the uploaded research report. Each entry explains how the pattern may feel, what may help and when to get support. Related concepts are not interchangeable, and none of these entries can assess you or your partner.

40 concepts

Attachment activation

Attachment protestDescriptive umbrella, not a diagnosis

How it may feel: A meaningful connection feels threatened and you may feel urgency to restore contact or reassurance. Behaviours can include repeated calls, checking, angry withdrawal or indirect tests.

What may help: Separate the observable event from the story you fear it means. Name the need and make one direct, proportionate request; allow time for a reply and respect boundaries.

When to seek support: Seek help if urges disrupt sleep, eating, study, work or safety. Threats, stalking and coercion require safety-focused support.

HyperactivationEstablished adult-attachment regulation framework

How it may feel: Attention, emotion and proximity-seeking intensify when support feels unavailable. You may monitor cues or seek reassurance repeatedly.

What may help: Pause before acting, ground yourself, reduce checking and make one clear request. Build more than one source of emotional support.

When to seek support: Seek help if the pattern feels uncontrollable or interferes with daily life.

DeactivationEstablished adult-attachment regulation framework

How it may feel: You may minimise needs, rely only on yourself, change the subject or withdraw when closeness feels overwhelming.

What may help: If safe, explain that the conversation feels intense, ask for space and agree when you can return. Share one manageable need.

When to seek support: Seek support if withdrawal repeatedly blocks communication or repair.

Attachment orientations

Anxious attachmentResearch dimension, not a diagnosis

How it may feel: You may be especially alert to rejection cues, uncertain about availability or seek reassurance more often under stress.

What may help: Notice triggers, distinguish facts from assumptions, state needs directly, maintain outside relationships and agree realistic communication expectations.

When to seek support: Consider counselling when fear, checking or reassurance seeking affects functioning.

Avoidant attachmentResearch dimension, not a diagnosis

How it may feel: You may feel uncomfortable depending on someone, find vulnerability difficult or prefer to manage distress privately.

What may help: Explain what kind of space helps and, if safe, set a time to reconnect. Share one small feeling or need without forcing disclosure.

When to seek support: Seek help when distance becomes distressing or repeatedly prevents repair.

Fearful attachmentSimplified combination of anxiety and avoidance dimensions

How it may feel: You may want closeness while also fearing rejection, dependence or hurt. It can feel like moving toward someone and then pulling away.

What may help: Move at a safe pace, clarify boundaries and needs, and look for consistent actions rather than trying to solve uncertainty through tests.

When to seek support: A professional can help when this conflict is distressing or linked to trauma. A quiz cannot assign this label.

Secure attachmentResearch orientation, not perfection or constant calm

How it may feel: Closeness and independence can both feel possible. You may ask for help, express needs and manage disagreements more flexibly.

What may help: Maintain mutual responsiveness, autonomy, honest communication and repair. Security is built through behaviour over time, not a permanent badge.

When to seek support: No treatment is implied. Support can still help through difficult periods.

Internal working modelAttachment-theory framework

How it may feel: Past experiences may shape expectations about yourself, other people and whether support will be available.

What may help: Treat your first interpretation as a hypothesis. Look for evidence, alternative explanations and repeated patterns. Reliable experiences and therapy can support change.

When to seek support: Seek support when old expectations cause persistent distress or relationship difficulty.

Relationship resources

Safe havenAttachment relationship function

How it may feel: A close person may feel like someone you can turn to for comfort during distress.

What may help: Ask for the support that helps: listening, practical help, reassurance or quiet company. Keep support diversified.

When to seek support: If the relationship is unsafe or support is unavailable, seek other trusted people or professional support.

Secure baseAttachment relationship function

How it may feel: A reliable relationship may help you feel supported while exploring, studying, working and pursuing goals.

What may help: Protect both connection and autonomy. Encourage each other's interests and friendships while keeping communication dependable.

When to seek support: Seek support if closeness and independence feel impossible to balance.

Attachment behaviours

Proximity seekingEstablished attachment behaviour

How it may feel: You may want more contact, conversation, affection or time together when distressed. Wanting closeness is a normal human need.

What may help: Ask directly for a specific form of contact, and discuss what is realistic for both people. A need for closeness does not override consent or space.

When to seek support: Seek support if contact attempts become repetitive, unwanted or difficult to control.

Separation distressAttachment-related response, not automatically a disorder

How it may feel: Distance or anticipated separation may bring sadness, anxiety, searching, restlessness or difficulty settling.

What may help: Use routines, supportive contact, grounding and mutually agreed plans. Respect separation and no-contact boundaries.

When to seek support: Seek assessment when distress is intense, persistent or affects functioning.

Cognitive and emotional processes

Reassurance seekingResearch construct; can become repetitive

How it may feel: You may repeatedly ask whether someone is angry, still cares or plans to stay. Reassurance may bring short relief, followed by renewed doubt.

What may help: Ask once clearly, listen to the answer and notice whether repeating the question keeps worry active. Practise tolerating uncertainty.

When to seek support: Consider therapy if checking or reassurance takes significant time or interferes with life; assess OCD/anxiety separately.

Rejection sensitivityResearch construct

How it may feel: You may expect rejection, notice possible signs quickly and react strongly to ambiguous cues. A delayed reply can feel like rejection when the reason is unknown.

What may help: Separate the event from its meaning. Consider alternatives and ask a calm clarifying question when appropriate.

When to seek support: Seek support if perceived rejection leads to conflict, isolation, intense distress or avoidance.

HypervigilanceDescriptive process; may overlap with trauma responses

How it may feel: You may scan tone, facial expressions, online status or small changes for signs of danger, rejection or conflict.

What may help: Notice the present, reduce repeated checking and identify whether there is an actual safety concern. Do not ignore genuine warning signs.

When to seek support: A trauma-informed clinician can help when alertness persists or disrupts sleep. If there is current danger, prioritise safety.

RuminationWell-studied cognitive process

How it may feel: The same event or question keeps replaying without new information. You may revisit a conversation or lose sleep trying to find certainty.

What may help: Write observable facts, one action you can take and what is outside your control. Shift to a chosen activity. CBT-based support may help.

When to seek support: Seek assessment if repetitive thinking takes hours, disrupts sleep or occurs with depression, anxiety or compulsive checking.

Intolerance of uncertaintyResearch construct

How it may feel: Not knowing what a partner thinks or when they will reply may feel unusually difficult, leading to checking or demands for certainty.

What may help: Practise tolerating small amounts of uncertainty without repeated checks. Agree reasonable communication expectations.

When to seek support: Professional support can help when uncertainty drives avoidance, compulsions or significant distress.

Emotional dysregulationBroad descriptive term, not a diagnosis

How it may feel: Emotions may feel unusually intense, last a long time or be hard to express safely. You may act quickly and regret it later.

What may help: Pause, name the emotion, notice body cues and choose a response after the peak has eased. Grounding, sleep, routines and therapy skills may help.

When to seek support: Seek help when emotions repeatedly lead to harmful conflict, self-harm risk or daily disruption.

Emotional floodingCouple-communication term / high-arousal state

How it may feel: During conflict your body may feel overwhelmed and it can be hard to listen, think clearly or respond flexibly.

What may help: Take a mutually understood pause, lower stimulation and agree a specific time to return. Do not use a break to punish or disappear.

When to seek support: If arguments regularly escalate or feel unsafe, seek individual support first.

MentalizationPsychological capacity / clinical framework

How it may feel: You may forget that your own and another person's thoughts and intentions are uncertain, especially under stress. Mind-reading can replace curiosity.

What may help: Ask: “What do I know, what am I guessing, and what could I ask?” Check interpretations without assuming you know the other person's mind.

When to seek support: Seek support if repeated misunderstandings and conflict are difficult to resolve.

Co-regulationRelational process; not one person's duty

How it may feel: A calm, respectful interaction may help both people settle. Tone, predictability and responsiveness can affect how safe a conversation feels.

What may help: Ask what support is welcome. Offer clear information and follow through. Both people remain responsible for their own behaviour.

When to seek support: If one person is expected to manage every emotion or is punished for not doing so, consider support and boundaries.

Couple interaction cycles

Pursuer–distancerDyadic interaction pattern

How it may feel: One person seeks discussion or closeness while the other seeks distance. Both may feel misunderstood as pursuit and withdrawal increase.

What may help: Name the cycle rather than labelling people. If safe, set a pause with a clear return time and discuss one issue at a time.

When to seek support: A couple therapist may help only when both people can participate freely and without fear or coercion.

Demand–withdrawResearch-backed dyadic communication pattern

How it may feel: One person presses for change or discussion while the other retreats. Each response may intensify the next.

What may help: Agree when to resume, use one clear request and listen to each person's concern. Avoid demanding immediate discussion or indefinite silence.

When to seek support: Seek help when the cycle is persistent. It does not prove cause or attachment style.

Protest polkaEmotionally Focused Therapy term for a negative interaction cycle

How it may feel: The couple may feel trapped in repeated pursuit and distance, while the original need becomes hard to hear.

What may help: Identify the shared cycle, the feelings underneath and the needs each person is trying to protect. A trained EFT professional can guide this.

When to seek support: Couple therapy is only appropriate where there is safety, consent and freedom to participate.

Negative reciprocityResearch concept in conflict communication

How it may feel: A critical or defensive response is followed by another negative response, so the interaction escalates.

What may help: Interrupt the exchange, return to one concrete issue, own your part and try a repair statement. No one has to accept insults or threats.

When to seek support: Professional support may help if arguments regularly escalate or feel unsafe.

StonewallingDescriptive conflict behaviour; motive requires context

How it may feel: Someone disengages during interaction. It may reflect overload, avoidance, a boundary or punishment; silence alone does not reveal the reason.

What may help: If you need a pause, say what is happening and when you can return. Respect another person's request for space.

When to seek support: Seek support if disengagement repeatedly prevents repair. If silence is used to intimidate or control, prioritise safety.

Angry withdrawalPossible protest behaviour; context matters

How it may feel: You may become distant to communicate hurt or hope the other person notices. It can also reflect overwhelm or a genuine boundary.

What may help: If safe, state the hurt or need directly. If you need space, explain the pause and return time. Avoid using silence to punish.

When to seek support: Seek support if withdrawal harms connection or is part of threats, intimidation or control.

Rupture, repair and loss

Attachment injuryRelational/clinical construct; not a psychiatric diagnosis

How it may feel: A major absence, betrayal or dismissal when support was expected may leave trust damaged. The event may be revisited whenever closeness is tested.

What may help: Repair may require acknowledgement, empathy, accountability, changed behaviour and time. Forgiveness or reconciliation cannot be demanded.

When to seek support: A couples clinician may help only if both people can participate safely. Individual support is appropriate when overwhelmed or unsafe.

RuptureRelational event / loss of trust or connection

How it may feel: A broken promise or argument may damage trust, leaving someone feeling unseen or abandoned.

What may help: Describe the event and its impact. Ask what acknowledgement or change is needed. An apology is a start, not proof that trust is repaired.

When to seek support: Seek support if the rupture remains unresolved or involves betrayal, abuse or safety concerns.

RepairRelationship process; not instant forgiveness

How it may feel: You may need acknowledgement, empathy, responsibility and evidence of changed behaviour before trust returns.

What may help: Discuss what happened, the impact, what each person owns, what needs to change and when to review it. Forgiveness does not require reconciliation.

When to seek support: A therapist can support repair when both people participate freely. Safety and accountability come first.

Ambiguous lossGrief framework

How it may feel: You may feel loss without clear closure, such as a person being absent but still psychologically present or the relationship status uncertain.

What may help: Name what is known and unknown, seek trusted support, create routines and choose boundaries that reduce repeated uncertainty.

When to seek support: Grief counselling may help when uncertainty remains deeply distressing or interferes with daily life.

Breakup griefCommon response to relationship loss; not automatically a disorder

How it may feel: You may miss someone, feel sadness, anger, relief, longing, disrupted sleep or appetite, or urges to check and reconnect. There is no single timetable.

What may help: Respect no-contact requests, reduce checking that reopens distress, keep basic routines and lean on people outside the relationship.

When to seek support: Seek professional support when grief remains overwhelming, daily functioning is seriously affected or safety is a concern.

Clinical overlaps and popular terms

Relationship OCDOCD symptom theme; diagnosis requires full assessment

How it may feel: Intrusive doubts about love or compatibility may trigger checking, comparing, reviewing memories or repeated reassurance seeking.

What may help: Do not use a website quiz to decide whether you have OCD. A clinician experienced in OCD can assess symptoms; CBT with exposure and response prevention may be discussed.

When to seek support: Seek assessment when obsessions or compulsions take substantial time, cause distress or disrupt functioning.

LimerenceEmerging research / descriptive term; not a formal diagnosis

How it may feel: You may experience intense, intrusive romantic preoccupation, especially where feelings are uncertain or unreciprocated.

What may help: Reduce reinforcing behaviours such as constant checking. Rebuild routines and outside connections; consider what the relationship actually offers.

When to seek support: Seek support if preoccupation feels uncontrollable or affects daily functioning. Research and treatment evidence remain developing.

Digital surveillanceDigital safety / privacy concern

How it may feel: A person may repeatedly check another person's location, messages, follows, likes or device activity. It can cross privacy boundaries and become coercive control.

What may help: Do not monitor or access devices without freely given consent. If you are being monitored, consider a safer device and specialist support.

When to seek support: Seek individual safety support if surveillance is unwanted, persistent or linked to threats, isolation or fear.

Jealousy inductionDescriptive behaviour; not a healthy relationship strategy

How it may feel: Someone may provoke jealousy to obtain proof of care or attention. It can create insecurity and conflict rather than trust.

What may help: Replace tests with a direct request for reassurance, clarity or commitment. Discuss concerns without monitoring or controlling someone.

When to seek support: Seek support if jealousy leads to surveillance, threats, isolation or repeated conflict.

Love bombingPopular term; not a formal diagnosis

How it may feel: Overwhelming early affection or attention may feel flattering, intense or confusing. Concern rises when it comes with pressure, boundary violations or control.

What may help: Take time, keep outside relationships and notice whether “no” is respected and actions remain consistent.

When to seek support: If affection is paired with intimidation, isolation, pressure or threats, seek private specialist support.

BreadcrumbingPopular social term; not a validated clinical construct

How it may feel: Intermittent low-effort contact may keep someone interested without clarity or commitment. You may feel hopeful, confused or stuck waiting.

What may help: Decide what consistency and clarity you need. Ask once about expectations, then choose boundaries based on actions, not promises alone.

When to seek support: Seek support if uncertainty and checking affect sleep, work, study or wellbeing.

Trauma bondingUse only in harmful dynamics involving abuse/control; not ordinary longing

How it may feel: A person may feel attached to someone who also causes fear, control, manipulation or harm. Alternating harm and relief can make separation confusing.

What may help: Prioritise individual, safety-focused support and trusted people. Do not frame this as an attachment-style problem or use routine couple communication exercises.

When to seek support: A domestic-violence or safeguarding specialist can help assess options and safety. Do not pressure someone to leave in a way that increases danger.

Safety and autonomy

Coercive controlPattern of behaviour restricting autonomy; safety issue

How it may feel: Repeated intimidation, threats, surveillance, isolation, pressure or control of money and choices may make someone feel afraid or unable to act freely.

What may help: Prioritise private, individual safety support. Avoid confrontation if it could increase danger. Routine joint therapy is not a default response.

When to seek support: Seek specialist support; call emergency services if in immediate danger. In India, 112 is the emergency route and 181 may provide support/referral.

From understanding to a next step

Three perspectives, three kinds of support

If you notice protest urges

  1. Name the observable trigger without adding an interpretation.
  2. Name the story you fear may be true, and treat it as a hypothesis.
  3. Regulate before acting: ground, take a pause or move a little.
  4. Make one direct, proportionate request.
  5. Respect the other person's boundaries and build support beyond one relationship.

If someone seeks reassurance from you

  1. Acknowledge the feeling without agreeing with an accusation you believe is inaccurate.
  2. Give clear, honest information when possible.
  3. If you need a pause, say when you can return.
  4. Follow through on what you agree.
  5. Keep boundaries around insults, threats, surveillance and unwanted contact.

If both of you are stuck in a cycle

  1. Name the cycle instead of labelling each other “anxious” or “avoidant”.
  2. Agree on reasonable contact expectations and what a time-out means.
  3. Return to one issue at a time.
  4. Discuss impact, responsibility and specific changes.
  5. Review whether the change happened. Safety and consent come first.
Example repair script from the research report

“When the conversation stopped without a plan to return, I felt worried and then pushed harder. I can see that the repeated messages made it harder to talk. I need us to agree on a pause with a clear return time. I will respect the pause, and I need us to come back to the issue rather than leave it unresolved.”

These steps are for relationships where both people can participate safely and voluntarily. Where there is intimidation, stalking, coercive control, sexual or physical violence, threats or fear of retaliation, prioritise individual safety and specialist support. Do not use attachment language to pressure someone to stay, respond, forgive or resume contact.

Words you can borrow

One clear request, without a test

Adapt this only if it feels safe and respectful for both people:

“When I don't hear back, I notice I start feeling uncertain. I don't expect an instant reply. Could we agree on how to let each other know when we're busy and when we can reconnect?”

For a pause during conflict:

“I want to understand this, but I'm getting overwhelmed. I need a break. Can we come back to this later today when we can both listen?”
