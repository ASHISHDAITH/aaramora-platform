A supportive practice · not a cure

Make a little room to breathe.

Gentle movement, comfortable breathing and guided rest may be useful parts of everyday wellbeing. Research on yoga and depression is encouraging in some comparisons, mixed in others, and does not show that a short website practice treats depression or replaces professional care.

Study findings · compared carefully

What research says, and what it does not.

Trials study different forms of yoga, people, lengths of practice and comparison groups. A result against usual care is not the same as a result against an active treatment. These summaries refer to the studies linked below, not to the specific practices on this page.

MODERATE EVIDENCE · SHORT TERM

Yoga for depression · 2013 review

12 trials · 619 people

The review found moderate evidence of a short-term reduction in depressive symptoms compared with usual care. Evidence against relaxation and aerobic exercise was rated limited. The included trials did not report safety data, and the review could not pool long-term effects.

Interpretation: promising as an add-on option in the studied settings; not proof of a cure or long-term effect. The review reported that none of its included trials provided safety data.

Read the 2013 review · PubMed
ASSOCIATION · NOT A PRESCRIPTION

Yoga in people with mental disorders · 2021 review

19 studies · 1,080 people

Thirteen studies (632 participants) entered the meta-analysis. Yoga was associated with greater reductions in depressive symptoms than waitlist, treatment as usual and attention control. Across studies, more sessions per week were associated with greater reductions; this is not proof that everyone should practise more often.

The review included adults with several diagnosed mental disorders, not depression alone. The higher-frequency finding is an across-study association, not a dose recommendation for an individual.

Read the 2021 review · PubMed
COMPARATOR AND OUTCOME MATTER · 2024 REVIEW

Yoga for depressive disorder · 2024 review

24 studies · 1,395 people

Depression severity: a short-term benefit was found versus passive controls (20 studies were meta-analysed), but the difference was not statistically significant versus active controls. Remission: the review reported higher remission rates versus both passive and active controls.

Evidence quality ranged from moderate to very low. Safety comparisons were very low quality and adverse-event reporting was heterogeneous. This review does not prove that every yoga practice is safe or that yoga replaces standard care.

Read the 2024 review · full text
ADDITIONAL REVIEW · 2023

Yoga for major depressive disorder

A separate review of major depressive disorder included 34 randomised trials with 2,341 participants in total: 1,269 in yoga groups and 1,072 in control groups. It reported moderate effects on depression measures in the pooled comparisons. The authors also reported a negligible effect on anxiety; do not treat that finding as evidence that yoga reliably treats anxiety.

This is a separate 2023 review, with a different search period and comparator mix from the 2024 review. Do not combine its participant totals or effect estimates with the other meta-analyses.

Read the 2023 review · PubMed

Optional · low pressure · no score

Choose a gentle starting point.

These are general comfort practices, not a prescribed programme. You do not need to practise every day or finish a session. Choose a position that is comfortable for you.

1–3 minutes · seated or standing

Small, comfortable movement

Keep movements within an easy range. Skip any movement that causes pain, strain, dizziness or unease.

  1. Settle your feet on the floor or find a stable standing position.
  2. Let your shoulders rest. Slowly roll them only if that feels comfortable.
  3. Turn your head a small amount from side to side, without forcing the stretch.
  4. Pause. Notice whether you would like to continue, change position or stop.

This is a simple mobility prompt, not a yoga therapy sequence.

1–2 minutes · natural breathing

Notice one comfortable breath

You do not need to take a deep breath or change your breathing pattern.

  1. Sit in a supported position, or stay as you are.
  2. Notice one ordinary breath coming in and going out.
  3. If it feels easy, allow the out-breath to finish without pushing it.
  4. Let your breathing return to its usual rhythm. You can stop at any time.

No breath holds, rapid breathing or forced counts.

5 minutes · guided rest draft

A short, Yoga Nidra-inspired pause

Find a supported position. You may keep your eyes open. Move or stop whenever you need to.

  1. Notice the surface supporting your body.
  2. Bring attention gently to the feet, then the legs, hands, arms, shoulders and face. You can skip any area.
  3. Notice sounds around you. You do not need to name or change them.
  4. Feel the support beneath you once more. When ready, look around and return to your day.

This short body-awareness script is an original relaxation draft. It is not a traditional full Yoga Nidra protocol and has not been tested as a treatment.

Draft transcript · English · not an audio recording

Words for a gentle pause

“You may rest in a position that feels supported. There is nothing to achieve here. Notice the places where your body meets the chair, bed or floor. If it feels comfortable, bring attention to your feet, then your legs, hands, arms and face. You can keep your eyes open, move at any time, or stop. Notice a sound in the room. Let your breathing do what it needs to do. When you are ready, look around and return to the next part of your day.”

Before you begin

Your comfort comes first.

Yoga and breathing exercises are not suitable for every person or every moment. You are allowed to skip them completely.

Pause or stop if

You feel dizzy, faint, unusually breathless, numb, in pain, panicky or more distressed. Return to ordinary breathing and a comfortable position. Seek medical help for severe, new or persistent symptoms.

Keep breathing unforced

This page does not teach rapid breathing, forceful breathing, breath retention, competitive breathing counts or intense poses. Do not push through discomfort to complete a session.

Check with a clinician first

If you are pregnant or have a medical condition, recent injury or movement restriction, ask a qualified clinician what kinds of movement or breathing practice are appropriate for you.

When another kind of support may help

If low mood, anxiety or sleep problems persist, become severe or affect daily life, a qualified professional can discuss care options. Yoga is not a substitute for assessment, therapy or prescribed treatment.

During an emergency or crisis: do not use a breathing exercise as the only response. In India, call 112 for an immediate emergency or contact Tele-MANAS at 14416 for mental-health support.
