A dedicated women's health corner

Your body, cycle and mind deserve to be heard.

Hormones, periods, sleep, pain and mental wellbeing can overlap. You deserve clear information without blame, myths or pressure to “fix” your body.

Start with what you notice, not a label. These topics are information pages, not symptom checkers or diagnoses. Similar symptoms can have different causes. A qualified health professional can help work out what is relevant to you.

HORMONES · METABOLIC · REPRODUCTIVE HEALTH

PMOS (formerly PCOS; often called PCOD)

PMOS is the new international name for the condition previously called polycystic ovary syndrome. It can affect menstrual cycles, androgen-related symptoms, metabolic health, fertility and emotional wellbeing.

You may still see “PCOS” on medical records and research, or hear “PCOD” in India. PCOD is a term you may hear in India, while international records and research may use PCOS or the updated PMOS name. Ask your clinician what features and criteria were used in your assessment.

How might this feel day to day?

Periods that are irregular or absent, acne, excess facial or body hair, scalp hair thinning, difficulty with ovulation or fertility, and metabolic concerns can occur. Not everyone has the same symptoms. It is not simply an “ovarian cyst” condition.

What may help?
  • A clinician-led assessment and an individual plan based on the person's priorities and symptoms.
  • Regular physical activity and a sustainable, balanced eating pattern can support metabolic health. Benefits can occur without weight loss.
  • Different medical options may be considered for cycle irregularity, androgen-related symptoms, metabolic risks or fertility goals.
  • Emotional wellbeing, sleep, body image and anxiety/depression deserve attention too.
When to seek support

Consider a clinician visit for persistently irregular or absent periods, troublesome hair or skin changes, fertility concerns, or metabolic health questions. The right evaluation depends on age, symptoms and medical history.

How can it affect mood, confidence or memory?

Some people report anxiety, low mood, body-image concerns, sleep difficulties or stress related to symptoms and fertility concerns. The 2023 international guideline recommends clinicians remain alert to depression and anxiety in people with PMOS/PCOS and assess them appropriately.

Midlife brain-health research is still developing. A 2024 cohort reported associations with some cognitive and white-matter measures, but it could not show that the syndrome caused those differences. A 2026 systematic review of 22 studies found small, statistically non-significant pooled associations in executive function, attention and working memory. This is not evidence that PMOS causes memory loss or brain damage.

If memory or concentration changes trouble you, discuss them with a clinician. Sleep, mood, anxiety, thyroid problems, anaemia, medicines and other factors may also matter.

PELVIC PAIN · MENSTRUAL HEALTH

Endometriosis

A long-term condition where tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows elsewhere. Pain can affect periods, daily activity, bowel or bladder function, sex and fertility.

How might this feel day to day?

Period pain that disrupts everyday life, ongoing pelvic pain, pain during or after sex, cyclical bowel or urinary symptoms, or fertility difficulties can be reasons to ask about endometriosis.

What may help?
  • Assessment by a gynaecology clinician; symptom relief and investigations can often proceed in parallel.
  • Depending on the person, options may include pain management, hormonal treatment, specialist pelvic-pain support or surgery.
  • A normal ultrasound does not always rule out endometriosis. Persistent symptoms still deserve follow-up.
When to seek support

Seek assessment when period-related pain affects work, study, relationships or daily life, or when there is persistent pelvic pain, pain with sex, or cyclical bowel/bladder symptoms.

CYCLE · BLEEDING · PAIN

Heavy or painful periods

Very heavy, unpredictable or painful bleeding is not something a person should have to silently tolerate. Possible causes include fibroids, adenomyosis, PMOS, endometriosis and bleeding disorders.

How might this feel day to day?

Changing period products every 1–2 hours, bleeding longer than 7 days, large clots, bleeding through clothes or bedding, pain that disrupts life, tiredness or shortness of breath are reasons to discuss heavy bleeding with a clinician.

What may help?
  • Track dates, bleeding pattern, pain and how symptoms affect daily life, if tracking feels comfortable.
  • A clinician may recommend a blood count and other tests based on the history and examination.
  • Depending on the cause and preferences, management may include medicines, hormonal options, treatment for iron deficiency or a specialist procedure.
Do fibroids always need treatment?

No. Fibroids are common and non-cancerous; some cause no symptoms. Treatment is considered when symptoms or complications need attention, and options depend on location, size, symptoms and pregnancy plans.

When to seek support

Seek assessment when bleeding is very heavy, lasts more than a week, period pain disrupts daily activities, or you feel faint, unusually tired or short of breath. Sudden severe pain or heavy bleeding with fainting needs urgent medical care.

CYCLE-LINKED MOOD · MENTAL HEALTH

PMS and PMDD

Premenstrual symptoms can include physical changes and mood shifts. PMDD is a more severe condition that can seriously affect daily life and relationships.

How might this feel day to day?

Symptoms may follow a repeating pattern before a period and improve after bleeding begins. PMDD can involve intense mood symptoms, irritability, anxiety or low mood. A pattern across cycles matters more than one difficult day.

What may help?
  • A symptom diary across cycles may help a clinician understand timing and impact.
  • Depending on assessment, evidence-based options can include psychological support/CBT, lifestyle support, hormonal treatment or prescribed medicines.
  • There is no need to dismiss severe symptoms as “just hormones”.
When to seek support

Seek support when cyclical symptoms disrupt work, study, relationships or daily life. Severe depression or thoughts of self-harm need prompt human support; use the emergency/support routes on this site if immediate help is needed.

MIDLIFE · SLEEP · MOOD · MEMORY

Perimenopause and menopause

Hormonal changes around menopause can affect periods, temperature regulation, sleep, mood, concentration, memory, joints and vaginal or urinary comfort.

How might this feel day to day?

Hot flushes, night sweats, sleep disruption, anxiety or low mood, brain fog, memory or concentration changes, joint aches, vaginal dryness or urinary symptoms can occur. Experiences vary widely.

What may help?
  • Discuss symptoms and health history with a clinician; options should reflect personal risks and preferences.
  • Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) can help some menopause symptoms after an individual benefits-and-risks discussion.
  • Menopause-specific CBT may help with some hot flushes, sleep problems and mood symptoms.
  • Vaginal or urinary symptoms have their own treatment options; ask rather than silently tolerate them.
What about memory?

Memory and concentration changes are reported during perimenopause and menopause, and sleep disruption can contribute. New, sudden, worsening or function-limiting cognitive symptoms should not automatically be attributed to hormones; seek an appropriate medical assessment.

When to seek support

Discuss symptoms that disrupt sleep, mood, work or daily life. New, sudden or worsening memory changes, bleeding after menopause, or symptoms that concern you should be assessed rather than automatically attributed to hormones.

ENERGY · TEMPERATURE · CYCLES

Thyroid conditions

Thyroid hormones affect many body systems. Underactive thyroid can be associated with fatigue, feeling cold, dry skin, hair thinning, menstrual changes and low mood.

How might this feel day to day?

These symptoms are common and can have many causes. Symptoms alone cannot confirm a thyroid condition; a clinician may use blood tests and medical history.

What may help?

When hypothyroidism is confirmed, it is commonly treated with prescribed thyroid hormone replacement and follow-up blood tests. Do not start or change thyroid medicine or iodine supplements without medical advice.

When to seek support

Speak with a clinician if persistent fatigue, temperature sensitivity, menstrual changes, neck swelling or unexplained weight changes concern you. Blood tests and clinical history are needed; symptoms alone cannot confirm a thyroid condition.

FATIGUE · HEAVY BLEEDING · NUTRITION

Iron deficiency and anaemia

Heavy menstrual bleeding can contribute to iron deficiency and anaemia. Low iron can affect energy and physical functioning, but fatigue has many possible causes.

How might this feel day to day?

Persistent tiredness, reduced exercise tolerance, dizziness, palpitations or shortness of breath can be reasons to seek medical assessment. Symptoms alone do not confirm iron deficiency.

What may help?

A clinician may recommend blood tests, investigate the cause of blood loss and advise iron treatment or dietary changes as appropriate. Avoid high-dose iron supplements without assessment; excess iron can be harmful.

When to seek support

Seek assessment for persistent tiredness, dizziness, palpitations, reduced exercise tolerance or breathlessness. Severe breathlessness, chest pain or fainting needs urgent medical assessment.

PREGNANCY · POSTPARTUM · EMOTIONAL HEALTH

Postpartum depression and anxiety

Depression or anxiety can begin during pregnancy or after birth. It is a health condition, not a personal failure and not a sign that someone does not love their baby.

Urgent warning: sudden confusion, hallucinations, delusions or unusually high/manic behaviour after birth may indicate postpartum psychosis. This is a medical emergency requiring same-day emergency assessment.

How might this feel day to day?

Persistent sadness, anxiety, hopelessness, guilt, difficulty sleeping even when there is a chance to rest, loss of interest or difficulty managing everyday tasks can need professional attention.

What may help?
  • Tell an obstetrician, family doctor or mental-health professional; screening should connect to proper assessment and follow-up.
  • Psychological therapies and, when clinically appropriate, medication can be considered with pregnancy and breastfeeding status included in the discussion.
  • Practical help, rest and supportive relationships can complement care, but should not replace it when symptoms are significant.
When to seek support and urgent help

Severe confusion, hallucinations, extreme agitation, inability to sleep for prolonged periods with unusual energy or beliefs, or thoughts of harming oneself or the baby need urgent medical help. Do not wait for a routine appointment.

Preparing for a women's health appointment

You do not need to remember everything. If it feels useful, write down a few points before the visit:

  • When symptoms began and how often they happen
  • Period dates, bleeding pattern and pain
  • Sleep, mood, energy and memory changes
  • Medicines and supplements you currently take
  • How symptoms affect work, study, relationships or daily life
  • Your questions, priorities and pregnancy plans if relevant

Ask: “What else could explain these symptoms?”, “What tests are actually useful for me?”, “What are the benefits, risks and alternatives for each option?”, and “When should I come back?”

Postpartum psychosis warning
