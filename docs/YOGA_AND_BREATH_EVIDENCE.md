# AARAMORA Yoga and Breath: Evidence and Editorial Register

**Research snapshot:** 24 November 2026  
**Public page:** `yoga-and-breath.html`  
**Status:** Initial draft. Not clinically or yoga-professionally approved.

This register separates findings from named studies, product design decisions and unverified ideas. It is not a clinical practice guideline and does not establish that the short practices on AARAMORA have been tested.

## Evidence summaries

### Yoga for depression: 2013 systematic review and meta-analysis

Cramer H, Lauche R, Langhorst J, Dobos G. *Yoga for depression: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Depression and Anxiety. 2013;30(11):1068–1083. PMID: 23922209.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23922209/

- 12 randomised trials, 619 participants.
- The review reported moderate evidence for short-term effects compared with usual care.
- It rated the evidence against relaxation and aerobic exercise as limited.
- The trials were heterogeneous and had methodological limitations; none reported safety data.
- Long-term effects could not be meta-analysed.

**AARAMORA wording:** One review found moderate evidence of short-term benefit compared with usual care, but only limited evidence compared with relaxation or aerobic exercise. The review could not establish long-term effects and the included trials did not report safety data.

### Yoga and depressive symptoms in people with mental disorders: 2021 review

Brinsley J et al. *Effects of yoga on depressive symptoms in people with mental disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* British Journal of Sports Medicine. 2021;55:992–1000. PMID: 32423912.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32423912/

- 19 studies (1,080 participants) were included in the review; 13 studies (632 participants) entered the meta-analysis.
- The review included adults with several DSM-recognised mental disorders, not depression alone.
- The meta-analysis found greater reductions than waitlist, treatment as usual and attention control.
- Greater reductions were associated with higher yoga-session frequency per week across studies (β = −0.44, p < 0.01).

**AARAMORA wording:** Across the included studies, more frequent sessions were associated with greater reductions. This is an across-study association, not evidence that every person should practise more often or a personalised dose recommendation.

### Yoga for major depressive disorder: 2023 review

Wu Y, Yan D, Yang J. *Effectiveness of yoga for major depressive disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Frontiers in Psychiatry. 2023;14:1138205. PMID: 37032928; PMCID: PMC10077871.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37032928/  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10077871/

- 34 randomised trials, 2,341 participants in total (1,269 in yoga groups and 1,072 in control groups).
- The review reported moderate effects on depression measures (BDI-II and HAMD) in pooled comparisons.
- The reported effect on anxiety measured with STAI was described as negligible.
- The authors reported no adverse events in the yoga groups, but this should not be generalised to all forms of yoga, all populations or unsupervised online practice.

**AARAMORA wording:** This separate review reported improvements in depressive symptoms in its studied major-depressive-disorder populations. It used a different set of trials and methods from the 2013 and 2024 reviews; do not pool their participant counts or effect estimates together.

### Yoga for depressive disorder: 2024 review

Moosburner A et al. *Yoga for Depressive Disorder: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.* PMID: 40226719; PMCID: PMC11919030.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40226719/  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11919030/

- 24 studies (1,395 participants) were included; 20 studies (1,333 participants) were meta-analysed.
- **Depression severity:** a short-term effect was statistically significant compared with passive controls (SMD = −0.43; 95% CI −0.80 to −0.07), but not compared with active controls (SMD = −0.22; 95% CI −0.67 to 0.23).
- **Remission:** higher remission rates were reported compared with passive controls (OR = 3.20; 95% CI 1.45–7.10) and active controls (OR = 2.04; 95% CI 1.13–3.69).
- **Evidence quality:** moderate for remission versus passive controls and low versus active controls; depression-severity evidence was very low quality for both comparator groups. Quality-of-life findings were heterogeneous.
- **Safety:** pooled comparisons did not show statistically significant differences, but certainty was very low and adverse-event reporting was heterogeneous. A non-significant difference is not proof of zero risk.

**AARAMORA wording:** Keep the outcomes separate. Depression severity improved versus passive but not active controls; remission rates were higher versus both. Certainty varied from moderate to very low, and the safety evidence is weak. This single review does not establish that every yoga practice is safe or that a brief, unsupervised website practice treats depression.

## Evidence badges: rule for this page

The checked repository did not contain the earlier A/B/C/D rubric referred to in the project discussion. Do not invent letter grades or imply a formal GRADE assessment. The page currently uses descriptive study-level labels such as:

- **Moderate evidence · short term:** only when that is the certainty language used by the cited review for the stated comparison.
- **Limited evidence:** only when that is the review's stated certainty for the named comparison.
- **Association · not a prescription:** for observational or across-study associations that do not establish a dose or individual causal effect.
- **Comparator matters:** when a finding differs between passive and active controls.
- **Evidence quality varies:** when a review reports a range of GRADE certainty.

Every badge must name the population, outcome, timeframe and comparator nearby. It describes the cited study/review, not the quality of AARAMORA's own practice script.

## Other evidence-informed models for future tools

### Friendship Bench, Zimbabwe

Chibanda D et al. *Effect of a Primary Care–Based Psychological Intervention on Symptoms of Common Mental Disorders in Zimbabwe: A Randomized Clinical Trial.* JAMA. 2016;316(24):2618–2626. PMID: 28027368.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28027368/  
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2594719

- Cluster randomised trial in 24 primary-care clinics; 573 people were randomised.
- The intervention comprised six individual problem-solving therapy sessions delivered by trained, supervised lay health workers, plus an optional six-session peer-support programme.
- At six months, depression symptoms were present in 13.7% of the intervention group and 49.9% of the enhanced-usual-care control group.
- The trial tested a human-delivered, culturally adapted, supervised service, not a self-guided website worksheet.

**Product boundary:** AARAMORA's proposed Problem Bench may be *inspired by the sequence of identifying a problem, exploring options, choosing a feasible step and reviewing it*. It must not be called Friendship Bench, problem-solving therapy, or an equivalent intervention. It must not imply the trial's outcomes apply to a website.

### Healthy Activity Programme, Goa, India

Patel V et al. *The Healthy Activity Program (HAP), a lay counsellor-delivered brief psychological treatment for severe depression, in primary care in India: a randomised controlled trial.* The Lancet. 2017. PMID: 27988143; PMCID: PMC5236064.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27988143/  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5236064/

- Individually randomised trial in ten primary health centres in Goa.
- Participants were adults aged 18–65 years with moderately severe to severe depression (PHQ-9 >14).
- At three months, remission (PHQ-9 <10) was observed in 147/230 (64%) in enhanced usual care plus HAP, compared with 91/236 (39%) in enhanced usual care alone.
- HAP was a structured psychological treatment delivered by trained lay counsellors, not a self-directed activity calendar.

### HAP 12-month follow-up

Weobong B et al. *Sustained effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of the Healthy Activity Programme...* PLOS Medicine. 2017. PMID: 28898283; PMCID: PMC5595303.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28898283/  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5595303/

- Follow-up included 247 participants assigned to HAP plus enhanced usual care and 248 to enhanced usual care alone.
- HAP participants maintained the gains recorded at treatment end through 12 months; remission remained higher than in enhanced usual care alone.
- This follow-up concerns the same structured, counsellor-delivered intervention model, not a consumer website activity planner.

**Product boundary:** The existing Little Garden may offer an optional activity-planning prompt. It must not be described as HAP, behavioural activation therapy, or clinically effective because it resembles one feature of a supervised treatment.

## WHO stress guide and licensing

https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240003927  
Rights and language details: https://www.who.int/publications/b/53604

- The guide is informed by evidence and extensive field testing, and includes practical self-help skills for coping with stress.
- The publication page identifies CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO.
- The licence allows copying, redistribution, translation and adaptation for non-commercial purposes when appropriately cited; translations/adaptations must be under the same or similar licence.

## Safety and scope

NCCIH, *Yoga: Effectiveness and Safety*: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yoga-effectiveness-and-safety

- Yoga is generally considered safe for healthy people when performed properly, but strains and other injuries can occur.
- Beginners should avoid extreme practices and forceful breathing.
- Pregnant people, older adults and people with health conditions or injuries may need modifications and should discuss individual needs with their health provider and a qualified instructor.
- Pregnancy guidance notes avoiding hot yoga and some positions/conditions unless appropriately modified.

AARAMORA's initial online practices deliberately avoid forced deep breathing, rapid breathing, breath holds, extreme poses and eyes-closed requirements. Visitors must be able to stop, change position, keep eyes open or skip everything. Stop if dizzy, faint, unusually breathless, in pain or more distressed. A short web script is not yoga therapy, a personalised exercise plan or an emergency intervention.
