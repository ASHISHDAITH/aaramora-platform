# Women's Health Corner — Research and Content Brief

**Research checked:** 23 November 2026  
**Status:** Evidence-informed content plan and prototype copy. Not clinical advice. Every patient-facing article requires a qualified reviewer, date, expiry/review date, and native-language review before publication.

## 1. Scope and product role

Add a distinct **Women's Health** corner to the platform. Its role is to connect physical, reproductive, hormonal and mental wellbeing information with practical routes to professional care. It is an information and navigation resource, not a diagnosis, telemedicine, prescribing or emergency service.

Include women and other people who may experience menstruation, pregnancy, postpartum changes or menopause. Use respectful, inclusive language. Do not assume all women menstruate, want children, or experience the same symptoms.

## 2. Important terminology update: PMOS (formerly PCOS)

On 12 May 2026, an international consensus process announced the name **Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS)** for the condition previously called polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). The change reflects the fact that the condition has endocrine, metabolic, reproductive and psychological dimensions, rather than being simply an ovarian cyst condition.

During the transition, older research, patient records, clinical codes and search behaviour will continue to use PCOS. The international guideline indicates that both names will coexist during a transition period; preserve both terms in search and page metadata. Recommended display label:

**PMOS (formerly PCOS)**

Search synonyms:
- PMOS
- PCOS
- Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome
- Polycystic ovary syndrome
- PCOS symptoms / PMOS symptoms
- PMOS memory / PCOS memory

Do not say that diagnostic criteria or all clinical management changed just because the name changed. The international guideline's update says the name changed; the underlying clinical recommendations and care concepts still require clinical interpretation and individualisation.

## 3. PMOS and midlife brain health: accurate interpretation

### Evidence
A 2024 analysis of the CARDIA Women's Study cohort examined cognitive performance and, in a smaller subset, MRI measures in midlife. Of 907 participants who completed cognitive testing, 66 met the study's PCOS criteria. The PCOS group scored lower on some tests involving attention/cognitive control, verbal learning and memory. In the MRI subset, 25 participants with PCOS showed lower fractional anisotropy in total white matter; the report did not describe abnormal white matter.

### What it does and does not mean
- This is an observational association in one cohort.
- It does not prove that PMOS causes memory loss, brain damage or dementia.
- It does not predict what will happen to an individual.
- The MRI subgroup was small; larger and more diverse longitudinal studies are needed.
- The finding does not justify fear-based content or routine brain scans for people with PMOS.

### Safe public-facing wording
“One midlife cohort study found associations between a history of PCOS (now called PMOS) and lower average scores on some attention, verbal-learning and memory tests. A smaller MRI group also showed a difference in one white-matter measure. The study cannot show that PMOS caused these differences or predict an individual person's future. If memory or concentration changes affect daily life, discuss them with a clinician. Sleep, mood, thyroid health, iron deficiency and other factors may also be relevant.”

Avoid:
- “PMOS damages your brain.”
- “PCOS causes dementia.”
- “You will lose your memory in midlife.”
- “Walking / green tea / flax seeds reverse brain changes.”

## 4. Priority condition library

### PMOS (formerly PCOS)
**Understand:** A complex endocrine and metabolic condition with varied reproductive, skin/hair, metabolic, sleep and psychological features. It is not defined simply by ovarian cysts.

**What may help:** Shared decision-making; sustainable physical activity and healthy eating patterns; screening and management of metabolic risk where appropriate; individual treatment for menstrual irregularity, androgen-related symptoms or fertility goals; attention to sleep, anxiety, depression, body image and disordered eating.

**Evidence caution:** International guidance supports lifestyle intervention for general and metabolic health, with benefits possible even without weight loss. It does not identify one preferred diet composition for PMOS outcomes. Do not market any single food, tea, supplement or exercise as “hormone balancing” or a cure.

### Endometriosis
**Understand:** A long-term condition where tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus; it can have physical, sexual, psychological and social effects.

**Possible signs to discuss:** Chronic pelvic pain; period pain affecting daily activity; deep pain during or after sex; cyclical bowel or urinary symptoms; or infertility in association with symptoms.

**What may help:** Gynaecology assessment; symptom and impact history; imaging and referral as indicated; options may include pain management, hormonal treatment, specialist pelvic-pain support or surgery depending on symptoms and priorities. NICE notes that a normal ultrasound does not exclude endometriosis when symptoms persist; specialist follow-up may still be appropriate.

### Heavy menstrual bleeding, fibroids and adenomyosis
**Understand:** Heavy bleeding or significant pain can be related to fibroids, adenomyosis, PMOS, endometriosis, bleeding disorders and other causes.

**Signs to discuss:** Changing a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours, bleeding more than 7 days, large clots, bleeding through clothes or bedding, activity limitation, persistent tiredness or breathlessness.

**What may help:** Clinical evaluation and a full blood count where indicated; treatment depends on cause, symptom burden, plans for pregnancy and preferences. Options can include medicines, hormonal treatments, iron treatment if deficiency is present, or specialist procedures. Not every fibroid needs treatment.

### PMS and PMDD
**Understand:** Premenstrual symptoms can include mood and physical changes. PMDD is a more severe premenstrual disorder with substantial impact on daily functioning.

**What may help:** A prospective symptom diary over cycles; clinician assessment; depending on symptoms and preferences, evidence-based options may include psychological counselling/CBT, exercise and nutrition support, hormonal or non-hormonal medicines. Do not imply that severe depression is “just hormones.”

### Perimenopause and menopause
**Understand:** Symptoms vary and can affect periods, hot flushes/night sweats, sleep, mood, joints, sexual wellbeing, vaginal/urinary health and concentration or memory.

**What may help:** Individual assessment and shared decisions. Options can include HRT for suitable people after benefits/risks discussion, menopause-specific CBT for some hot flushes, sleep or mood symptoms, and separate care for genitourinary symptoms. Do not present HRT as suitable for everyone or herbal supplements as proven and risk-free.

### Thyroid conditions
**Understand:** Hypothyroidism can be associated with fatigue, cold intolerance, weight change, dry/thinning hair, menstrual irregularity, fertility issues and low mood. Symptoms overlap with many other conditions.

**What may help:** Medical history and appropriate thyroid blood tests. Confirmed hypothyroidism is commonly managed with prescribed thyroid hormone replacement and follow-up. Do not diagnose from symptoms or suggest self-adjusting medication or iodine supplements.

### Iron deficiency and anaemia
**Understand:** Heavy menstrual bleeding can lead to iron deficiency anaemia. Symptoms may include fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, dizziness, palpitations or shortness of breath, but symptoms are not diagnostic.

**What may help:** Clinician assessment, blood testing, identifying and treating the cause of blood loss, and iron replacement or diet advice when indicated. Do not encourage high-dose iron without assessment.

### Postpartum depression, anxiety and psychosis
**Understand:** Depression or anxiety can begin during pregnancy or in the first year after birth. It is a treatable health concern, not a personal failure. Postpartum psychosis is rare but is a medical emergency.

**What may help:** Screening linked to proper assessment and follow-up; psychological therapy and/or medication when clinically indicated; practical and social support as an addition, not a substitute for care.

**Urgent red flags:** Sudden confusion, hallucinations, delusions, mania or imminent risk of harm after childbirth need urgent same-day medical assessment / emergency care.

## 5. Fact-check of viral “hormone balance” claims

Do not reproduce the attached social-media list as medical advice.

| Claim | Evidence-safe interpretation |
|---|---|
| Walking / strength training balances PMOS hormones | Physical activity is recommended as part of healthy lifestyle and can improve metabolic health. It should not be described as a guaranteed hormone-balancing cure. |
| Whole foods, protein and fibre balance PMOS hormones | A balanced eating pattern supports general health. No single diet composition is proven superior for PMOS outcomes. |
| Green tea balances PMOS hormones | Not established as a stand-alone PMOS treatment in the international guideline. |
| Flax seeds balance PMOS hormones | Do not present as proven hormone treatment or a replacement for care. |
| Magnesium balances PMOS hormones | Do not present as a proven PMOS treatment without a specific clinician-identified indication. |
| Vitamin D balances PMOS hormones | Do not promote routine supplementation as hormone therapy. Deficiency and supplementation decisions should be based on individual clinical context. |
| Quality sleep and stress management balance hormones | Sleep and emotional wellbeing matter for overall health and symptom coping, but do not replace assessment or treatment of PMOS. |

## 6. Women's health and mental wellbeing

The corner should explicitly discuss that physical and mental symptoms can overlap without assuming one causes the other:
- Menstrual-cycle mood changes and PMDD.
- Sleep disruption and menopause-related mood/concentration symptoms.
- Body image, fertility stress and the emotional burden of a long diagnostic journey.
- Chronic pelvic pain, pain during sex, social withdrawal and work/study impact.
- Anxiety/depression screening in PMOS care.
- Perinatal depression/anxiety and urgent postpartum psychosis pathways.

Do not label any mood symptom as hormonal without assessment. Do not turn the section into a diagnostic quiz.

## 7. Appointment preparation tool

Offer a private, optional checklist / printable worksheet. It should not require registration and should not transmit data:
- When symptoms started and how often they occur.
- Period dates, cycle pattern, bleeding and pain.
- Sleep, mood, energy and cognitive changes.
- Medicines and supplements.
- How symptoms affect work, study, relationships and daily life.
- Pregnancy / fertility priorities if the person wants to discuss them.
- Questions: What else could explain this? Which tests are useful for me? What are the benefits, risks and alternatives? When should I follow up?

For v1, do not store this information on a server or send it to AI services. If local saving is later added, make it explicit, explain shared-device risks, and provide delete/export controls.

## 8. Clinical governance and launch gates

- Every article names a qualified reviewer, date reviewed, sources and next review/expiry date.
- PMOS terminology checked against the current international guideline and WHO terminology update.
- Local clinical lead verifies India-specific referral and treatment pathways.
- Hindi, Marathi and Romanised Hindi copy reviewed by native speakers; clinical copy also reviewed by a clinician fluent in the language where possible.
- Health content is distinct from generic wellbeing tips.
- No medicine names/doses framed as personal recommendations.
- No supplement regimen, “hormone reset”, detox, fertility promise, or weight-loss promise.
- Red-flag content routes to human care and is reviewed by the clinical lead.
- Re-check links and current guidance before publication and on the scheduled review date.

## 10. Evidence limits

- Evidence quality varies by condition and treatment; a guideline recommendation does not mean every intervention is suitable for every person.
- Some observational brain-health results are preliminary and cannot show causation.
- Website copy is not ready for clinical publication until reviewed by a qualified clinician.
- The site should be explicit about the limits of evidence rather than presenting every possible mechanism or supplement claim as established fact.
