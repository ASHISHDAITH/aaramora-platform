# Relationships Corner — Research and Content Brief

**Research review date:** 24 November 2026  
**Status:** Evidence-informed educational draft. Not medical advice, a therapy protocol or a validated screening resource. Qualified clinical and safeguarding review is required before public release.

## 1. What this corner is for

The Relationships corner helps visitors understand common relationship patterns, reflect on their own behaviour, identify boundaries and find appropriate human support. Topics include attachment protest, attachment anxiety and avoidance, pursue–withdraw cycles, conflict repair, consent, digital boundaries, breakups and family relationships.

The product is an information and self-reflection resource. It does not diagnose attachment styles, personality disorders or abuse; assess a partner; determine compatibility; tell someone whether to stay or leave; provide relationship counselling; or contact another person.

Use inclusive examples. Do not assume a heterosexual couple, marriage, monogamy, a particular gender role or one cultural model of family.

## 2. Attachment theory: the careful version

Adult romantic attachment is commonly studied along two dimensions:

- **Attachment anxiety:** heightened concern about rejection, abandonment or another person's availability. Under stress, someone may seek reassurance, monitor signs of connection or become more sensitive to delayed responses.
- **Attachment avoidance:** discomfort with dependence or emotional closeness. Under stress, someone may reduce emotional disclosure, distance themselves or withdraw.

These are research dimensions, not diagnoses or moral categories. They are not fixed identities, and they do not explain every behaviour. A person's responses can vary across relationships, situations and time. Context matters: actual inconsistency, a partner's behaviour, culture, practical constraints, trauma, stress and other factors can shape the interaction.

The 2022 review by Overall, Pietromonaco and Simpson discusses how attachment insecurity can affect responses in couple and family relationships, while partner responsiveness can sometimes buffer these responses. It supports explaining patterns and mutual responsiveness, not assigning identities from a short quiz.

## 3. Attachment protest: what the term means here

“Attachment protest” is not a DSM diagnosis and it is not a single standardised clinical test. Aaramora uses it as a plain-language educational phrase for behaviour that may occur when a person feels that connection or a partner's availability is threatened and tries urgently to restore contact, attention or reassurance.

Possible behaviours can include:
- repeated messages or calls after an unanswered message;
- repeatedly checking online status, read receipts or social media clues;
- indirect tests, hints or jealousy-provoking behaviour instead of a direct request;
- angry words or threats to end a relationship used to elicit reassurance;
- going quiet, sulking or withdrawing in the hope the other person will pursue.

Research discusses reassurance-seeking and attachment-related responses to relationship threat. The term does not explain every instance of repeated contact, anger or withdrawal. The same action may reflect a real pattern of unreliability, an unmet practical need, an attempt to set a boundary, or another context.

**Important balance:** asking for a reasonable response or feeling distressed by genuine unreliability is not automatically “protest behaviour”. Attachment-related distress also does not justify repeated unwanted contact, threats or ignoring another person's clear request for space. Both people's boundaries matter.

## 4. The pursue–withdraw loop

A pursue–withdraw pattern describes an interaction in which one person presses for discussion, closeness or resolution while the other pulls away or ends the conversation. Each response can intensify the other. It is a relationship interaction pattern, not proof that one person is “anxious” and the other is “avoidant”.

A 2024 study examined demand/withdraw behaviours and attachment in 63 cisgender, heterosexual couples during a moderate conflict discussion. Its sample and setting limit generalisation. Use it to illustrate one possible pattern, not as a universal rule.

For situations without fear, coercion or violence, a possible interruption is:
1. Name the loop without labelling or blaming either person.
2. Pause if either person is overwhelmed.
3. If safe and mutually agreed, state when the conversation will resume.
4. Return to one specific issue.
5. Describe impact, listen and make one concrete request.
6. Acknowledge impact and make a repair attempt where appropriate.

A pause is not the same as indefinite silent treatment. A request for space should be respected. These steps are not an intervention for abuse or coercive control.

## 5. Conflict education: useful language, not a verdict

The Gottman Institute's educational framework describes criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling as destructive conflict behaviours. Aaramora may explain the concepts in original language and describe more respectful alternatives. It must not use them to diagnose a partner, score a couple, predict divorce or claim that one behaviour proves a relationship is doomed.

For ordinary, non-abusive conflict, educational alternatives can include:
- describing one observable event rather than attacking character;
- saying how it affected you and why it matters;
- making one specific request;
- acknowledging the part you can own;
- asking for a pause with a clear return point, if safe;
- checking what you understood and what you missed.

## 6. Boundaries, consent, trust and digital behaviour

Content should cover:
- consent and the right to change one's mind;
- privacy, personal devices and passwords;
- location sharing, read receipts and social media;
- time apart, friendships and personal interests;
- money, work, study and family expectations;
- jealousy versus controlling behaviour;
- respecting a breakup, no-contact request or request for space.

Respectful connection includes autonomy, consent, privacy and the freedom to maintain relationships and interests outside the couple. Monitoring, forced access to devices, forced location sharing, isolation, sexual pressure or controlling finances must never be framed as proof of love.

## 7. Safety comes before relationship repair

The safety check on `relationships.html` appears before quizzes and communication advice. It asks about fear of a partner's reaction, monitoring/isolation/control, sexual pressure, threats, stalking and physical harm. It is **not** a validated intimate-partner-violence assessment, risk score or certification that a relationship is safe.

If someone selects **Yes** or **Not sure**, the page:
- encourages private, trusted or specialist support;
- keeps emergency and relevant support links visible;
- does not route them to couple communication exercises;
- does not recommend couples counselling as the default;
- does not ask for incident details, screenshots or names;
- warns that Quick Exit does not erase browser history, device records or network logs.

The WHO clinical handbook on care for women subjected to intimate-partner or sexual violence prioritises immediate emotional/psychological needs, immediate physical needs, ongoing safety and ongoing support. WHO first-line support uses the LIVES approach: Listen, Inquire about needs and concerns, Validate, Enhance safety and Support.

The National Domestic Violence Hotline (US) explains why couples therapy can be unsafe as a default in abusive relationships: an abuser may use disclosures against the other partner, and joint therapy does not correct unequal power. This is a safety reference, not an India referral.

### India support routes currently shown in the prototype
- **112:** pan-India emergency response number.
- **181:** Women Helpline. The Ministry of Women and Child Development describes a 24-hour support and referral service; current operational coverage can vary by State/UT and must be checked before release. Someone may call on a woman's behalf.
- **14416:** Tele-MANAS, a free 24/7 mental-health support route. It is not a specialist domestic-violence service.

Do not imply that any one number solves every situation. Do not add other NGOs or contacts from memory. Any new service requires an official source, named owner, last-verified date and next review date.

## 8. What may help: separate education from therapy

For relationship dissatisfaction where both people can participate freely and there is no coercion or fear, educational materials may help someone prepare for a conversation or decide whether to seek professional support.

A 2021 systematic review examined nine studies of four online relationship-education programmes involving more than 2,000 couples; findings were promising, but the evidence base was limited to a small number of programmes and studies.

A 2022 review of couple-therapy research from 2010–2019 identified several approaches meeting its review criteria. A 2026 update using different criteria found no model met its threshold for strong support, with seven models having modest support. This illustrates that evidence and review standards evolve. Aaramora should not promise any one method works for everyone.

The platform may:
- explain relationship concepts in plain language;
- provide a structured conversation worksheet;
- encourage individual counselling when distress persists or affects functioning;
- explain that couples therapy may be considered only when both people can participate safely and freely.

It must not provide therapy, mediate a relationship, contact a partner, or give personalised treatment recommendations.

## 9. Reflection tools: design and limitations

Two original, unvalidated reflection quizzes are implemented in `relationships.html`:

1. **Attachment protest reflection:** asks about repeated contact, checking for clues, reading delays as rejection, indirect testing or withdrawal, high-intensity protest words and difficulty focusing while waiting.
2. **Conflict pattern reflection:** asks about pursuing, withdrawing and repair/pause behaviours.

The separate safety check is not a scored quiz.

Rules:
- no numerical score, cutoff, diagnosis or attachment-style label;
- no partner score, compatibility result, prediction or stay/leave advice;
- results list only the behaviours the visitor marked Sometimes or Often;
- answers remain in the open page and are not saved, sent or analysed;
- no login, free-text history or incident disclosure is required;
- all prompts can be skipped or reset;
- no psychometric instrument is reproduced.

The quizzes are original educational prompts and have not been validated. A clinician and safeguarding specialist must review them before public release. Do not reproduce ECR-R or another proprietary/validated instrument unless licensing and governance are confirmed.

## 11. Copyright and research integrity

Do not scrape and republish complete books, paywalled articles, blogs, websites, worksheets, competitor pages, quiz questions, images or videos. Do not copy proprietary tests or commercial programme materials. Study sources lawfully, record bibliographic details and reuse licences, then write original explanations in Aaramora's own words. A link does not grant permission to reproduce a work.

A broad research review cannot honestly claim to have exhausted every book, blog, article or site. The list above is a curated starting bibliography; expand it through a documented literature search and clinical review rather than bulk copying.

## 12. Clinical and safeguarding release gates

- Qualified relationship/couples clinician reviews definitions, examples, quiz prompts and self-help suggestions.
- Domestic-violence/safeguarding specialist reviews the safety check and referrals.
- Native Hindi, Marathi and Romanised Hindi reviewers review translations; clinical wording should also be reviewed by a clinician fluent in the language where possible.
- Revalidate 112, 181, 14416 and State/UT routing before launch.

- Paid lived-experience co-design; never ask participants to disclose traumatic events to test the website.
- Complete keyboard, screen-reader, mobile, privacy and safety testing before public release.
