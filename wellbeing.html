Mental wellbeing · plain language, no labels

What might be happening, and what could help?

You may recognise a feeling without knowing its name. These guides describe common experiences, practical support options and when to speak with a professional. Similar symptoms can have different causes; this page cannot diagnose you.

How it may feel · what may help · when to seek support

The aim is not to label you. It is to help you recognise experiences, find a useful next step and connect with qualified care when needed.

A note about “healing”. There is no single exercise that cures depression, anxiety, trauma, panic or sleep problems. Many conditions have effective treatments and support, but the right plan depends on the person. Self-care can support recovery; it does not replace medical or psychological care when that is needed. Do not start, stop or change medication or supplements based on this page.
MOOD · ENERGY · INTEREST

Depression

How it may feel

A low or empty mood, loss of interest or pleasure, tiredness, hopelessness, guilt, slowed thinking, sleep/appetite changes or difficulty doing ordinary tasks. People do not all experience the same symptoms. It is more than a bad day and can affect work, study and relationships.

What may help

A clinician can assess what is happening and discuss evidence-based options such as behavioural activation, CBT, interpersonal therapy or other psychological care. Medication may be considered by a qualified prescriber depending on severity and preference. Small routines, connection and gentle activity can support care, but they are not a substitute when symptoms are persistent or severe.

When to seek support

Arrange a professional assessment when symptoms persist, keep returning or interfere with daily life. Seek urgent human help if you may harm yourself, cannot keep yourself safe or are unable to care for basic needs. Use the support banner for India routes.

WORRY · TENSION · ALERTNESS

Anxiety and persistent worry

How it may feel

Worry that feels hard to switch off, restlessness, irritability, muscle tension, difficulty concentrating, sleep disruption, stomach discomfort, sweating, shaking or a racing heart. Some people avoid situations that bring fear. These symptoms can have physical as well as psychological causes.

What may help

A trained professional may offer CBT or another evidence-based psychological intervention. Gradual exposure can help with some anxiety problems when planned safely with a professional. Regular meals, movement, reducing excess caffeine and alcohol, supportive contact and stress-management practice may help alongside care.

When to seek support

Talk with a health professional when anxiety is persistent, difficult to control or limiting your life. New chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or sudden physical symptoms need medical assessment rather than assuming anxiety.

SUDDEN SURGE · BODY ALARM

Panic attacks

How it may feel

A sudden rush of intense fear or discomfort with symptoms such as a racing heart, sweating, trembling, dizziness, breathlessness, chest discomfort, nausea, tingling or feeling unreal or out of control. It can feel frightening. Similar symptoms can also occur with medical problems.

What may help

When symptoms begin, move to a safe place, sit or stand comfortably, notice your feet and surroundings, and breathe gently and slowly without forcing deep breaths. Remind yourself that the surge can settle. If attacks recur, CBT-based care and a medical assessment can help identify the right support.

When to seek support

Do not self-diagnose a first or unusual episode. Seek urgent medical help for severe/new chest pain, collapse, severe breathing difficulty, one-sided weakness or other alarming symptoms. Repeated unexpected attacks or ongoing fear of another attack deserve clinical assessment.

OVERLOAD · TENSION · TOO MUCH

Acute stress surges (sometimes called a “stress attack”)

How it may feel

A person may describe a sudden wave of overwhelm, racing thoughts, tight muscles, irritability, shaking, a fast heartbeat, stomach symptoms or difficulty deciding what to do. “Stress attack” is an everyday phrase, not a formal diagnosis; the experience can overlap with panic, anxiety or physical illness.

What may help

Reduce the immediate demand where possible. Try orienting to the room, naming a few things you can see and hear, taking one manageable next step, drinking water if appropriate, and contacting someone steady. WHO's stress guide teaches grounding, noticing thoughts and acting on personal values. Longer-term support may include problem-solving, workload changes and professional help.

When to seek support

Get medical help for new or severe physical symptoms. Talk to a professional when stress persists, affects sleep/eating/functioning, or feels impossible to manage. If it is linked to unsafe work, violence or another ongoing threat, focus on safety and practical support, not just relaxation.

MEMORIES · AVOIDANCE · ALERTNESS

Trauma-related distress and PTSD

How it may feel

After a frightening or overwhelming event, some people experience intrusive memories or nightmares, avoidance of reminders, feeling constantly on guard, irritability, emotional numbness, shame, sleep problems or difficulty feeling safe. Having a response does not automatically mean someone has PTSD.

What may help

Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR are examples of treatments delivered by trained professionals for some people with PTSD. A clinician can help decide what fits. Stabilising routines, practical safety, supportive people and choice about what to share matter. No one should be pressured to recount traumatic details to use this website.

When to seek support

Consider professional support when symptoms persist, cause distress or interfere with life. Seek urgent help if safety is at risk, or if there is severe confusion, inability to function or thoughts of self-harm.

REPEATING THOUGHTS · MENTAL LOOPS

Overthinking and rumination

How it may feel

The same question, memory or imagined future keeps returning. You may replay conversations, search for certainty, check for reassurance or find it hard to sleep, concentrate or move to the next task. Thinking carefully is different from a loop that gives no new information.

What may help

Name the loop without arguing with yourself. Separate what you know from what you fear. Write one concrete action you can take and one part you cannot control. Try returning attention to a chosen task or activity. CBT-based support can help when rumination is persistent or tied to anxiety or depression.

When to seek support

Ask for help when repetitive thinking takes up large parts of the day, drives compulsive checking, disrupts sleep or relationships, or sits alongside depression, anxiety or trauma symptoms. Repetitive thoughts alone do not prove OCD.

NIGHTS · REST · DAYTIME ENERGY

Sleep difficulties and insomnia

How it may feel

Difficulty falling asleep, waking repeatedly, waking too early or feeling unrefreshed can leave someone tired, irritable, foggy or less able to manage daily tasks. Sleep symptoms can be linked with stress, mood, pain, medicines, breathing problems and other health conditions.

What may help

A regular wake time, a wind-down routine, reducing late caffeine, and a bedroom that supports rest may help. CBT for insomnia (CBT-I) is an evidence-based treatment. A clinician can check for other sleep or medical problems. Do not start or combine sleep medicines or supplements without checking with a clinician or pharmacist.

When to seek support

Seek assessment when sleep problems last for months, affect daily functioning or do not improve with basic changes. Ask about unusual sleep episodes, loud snoring/gasping, severe daytime sleepiness or sudden changes in sleep.

EXHAUSTION · DISTANCE · WORK

Work-related burnout

How it may feel

WHO describes burnout specifically in the occupational context: exhaustion, increased mental distance or cynicism about work, and reduced professional efficacy after chronic workplace stress. It is an occupational phenomenon, not a medical diagnosis, and should not be used as a catch-all for every kind of exhaustion.

What may help

The response needs to include the work conditions: workload, control, staffing, unfair treatment, support and recovery time. Set realistic boundaries where possible, discuss adjustments with a trusted manager or representative, and seek professional care if depression, anxiety or sleep symptoms are also present.

When to seek support

Talk with a clinician if symptoms affect health or functioning. Seek workplace, union, HR or legal advice where conditions are unsafe or discriminatory. Rest alone cannot fix an unchanged harmful workload.

MISSING · CHANGE · DISCONNECTION

Grief, loneliness and loss

How it may feel

Grief may come in waves: sadness, longing, anger, numbness, guilt, sleep or appetite changes, difficulty concentrating or a sense that the world has changed. Loneliness can happen even around other people. There is no single correct timetable.

What may help

Small routines, time with a trusted person, cultural or spiritual practices that feel right, support groups and space to remember can help. Counselling can provide room to make sense of a difficult loss. You do not need to force yourself to “move on” on someone else's schedule.

When to seek support

Seek support when grief feels unmanageable, daily functioning remains severely affected, or depression, trauma symptoms or safety concerns appear. Sudden loss, violence or complicated circumstances may need specialised support.

UNWANTED THOUGHTS · RITUALS · CHECKING

OCD-related intrusive thoughts and compulsions

How it may feel

Some people with OCD experience unwanted, distressing thoughts, images or urges and feel driven to repeat actions or mental rituals to reduce anxiety or prevent a feared outcome. Not every intrusive thought or repeated habit is OCD, and thoughts do not equal intentions.

What may help

A qualified clinician can assess the full pattern. CBT that includes exposure and response prevention (ERP) is an established treatment; medication may be discussed with a prescriber. Avoid turning reassurance, repeated checking or online quizzes into another ritual.

When to seek support

Seek assessment if obsessions, compulsions, avoidance or reassurance-seeking take substantial time, cause distress or interfere with school, work, relationships or daily life.

When several things overlap

Low mood, panic-like symptoms, poor sleep, appetite changes, fatigue, memory concerns and relationship distress can overlap with physical health conditions, medicines, grief, trauma or stressful circumstances. A clinician can help sort through possible causes. You do not need to arrive with the correct label or tell the whole story at once.

How to prepare for an appointment

Write down when symptoms began, how often they happen, what makes them better or worse, how sleep/eating/work/study/relationships are affected, medicines or supplements you use, and the questions you want answered. You may take a trusted person if you choose.
